A bounded project is one somebody already worked out. How the parts go together, what thickness holds up, where the fasteners land — that's settled. It doesn't get asked again.

What's left is the part only you can answer: how big is your space, and what should it look like. Answer about ten questions and you get a list of parts, cut to your numbers.

You're not designing furniture. If a question needs a woodworker to answer it, we shouldn't be asking you.

› Picnic tables

Picnic tables

Off the shelf, three choices. You have a tape measure.

Attached bench. The one everybody pictures. Seats hang off the same A-frames as the top, so it's one object — sit down and the whole thing stays put. Nothing to line up on assembly.

Separate benches. A table and two benches. Each stands on its own, so you can pull a bench out, put one against a wall, or seat someone in a wheelchair at the end. More pieces, more legs.

Frames only. Either shape, minus the long boards. The difficult frame geometry stays on one side; straight boards stay explicitly on your supply list.

Why anyone picks frames only
A long table can exceed what a local rack or fulfillment path can reasonably provide as finished straight boards. The hard geometry can still remain local while the long members come from somewhere else.

Every one of these is cut to a length you pick. Structural, Store, material, machine and commercial answers remain separate.

What the words mean ↗
A-frameThe pair of angled legs and cross-members that form one support station.
Frames onlyThe hard geometry is in the provided scope; long straight members remain holder supplied.
Cut listThe explicit list of parts and dimensions associated with the chosen definition.
KerfMaterial removed by a cutting tool. No current machine assumption is made by this preview.
